The Apollo Program — A Short History

The Apollo program was NASA's third human spaceflight program, running
from 1961 to 1972. Its goal, set by President John F. Kennedy in May
1961, was to land a man on the Moon and return him safely to Earth
before the end of the decade.

Key missions
Apollo 1 (1967) ended in tragedy when a cabin fire during a launch
rehearsal killed astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger Chaffee.

Apollo 8 (December 1968) was the first crewed mission to orbit the Moon.
Its astronauts — Frank Borman, Jim Lovell, and William Anders — took the
famous "Earthrise" photograph.

Apollo 11 (July 1969) achieved the goal. Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin
landed the lunar module Eagle in the Sea of Tranquility on July 20,
while Michael Collins remained in orbit aboard the command module
Columbia. Armstrong's first step was watched live by an estimated 600
million people.

Apollo 13 (April 1970) suffered an oxygen tank explosion en route to
the Moon. The crew — Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert, and Fred Haise — used the
lunar module as a lifeboat and returned safely. The phrase "Houston,
we've had a problem" entered popular culture.

Apollo 17 (December 1972) was the final crewed Moon landing. Eugene
Cernan and Harrison Schmitt (the only professional geologist to walk on
the Moon) spent about 75 hours on the surface.

Legacy
In total, twelve astronauts walked on the Moon across six landing
missions. The program cost roughly $25 billion (about $150 billion in
modern dollars) and returned 382 kilograms of lunar samples.
